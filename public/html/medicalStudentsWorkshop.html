EYE DISEASE & BLINDNESS · HIGH V LOW RESOURCE SETTINGS

Blindness

Most blindness is treatable or preventable, but the route to early diagnosis and effective care is unequal.

01

Major causes can be acted on

Each pages build this chart one cause at a time. It is a conceptual view rather than a table of exact percentages.

  • Refractive error
  • Cataract
  • Glaucoma
  • Diabetic retinal disease
  • Trachoma
  • Trauma and infection
  • Retinoblastoma and other causes
02

Refractive error

Corrective glasses are a highly cost-effective treatment for a major cause of visual impairment.

03

Cataract

Cataract surgery is a cost-effective treatment that can restore sight when patients reach a surgical service.

04

Glaucoma

Glaucoma needs early detection. Once advanced disease presents with severe sight loss, treatment cannot recover the vision already lost.

05

Diabetic retinal disease

Like glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy can be difficult to treat after late presentation. Screening allows timely laser or anti-VEGF treatment when indicated.

06

Trachoma

Good hygiene, antibiotics and surgery work together in the SAFE strategy to prevent infectious blindness from trachoma.

  • Surgery
  • Antibiotics
  • Facial cleanliness
  • Environmental improvement
07

Trauma and infection

Occupational health strategies, public education and early treatment can prevent severe sight loss from injury or infection.

08

Retinoblastoma

Routine fundal reflex examination gives a rare but dangerous childhood cancer a chance to be found early.

09

Why absolute blindness can still grow

Eye services are expanding, but population growth and demographic change can grow faster. The mismatch leads to an increasing absolute number of people living with blindness.

Need tends to increase as populations become more disadvantaged, while service availability can fall. The gap becomes unmet need, even within high-resource countries.

ANATOMY & PHYSIOLOGY OF VISION

Visual System

Vision begins when light is focused onto the retina and continues as signals travel through the optic pathways to the visual cortex.

01

From light to retina

The cornea and lens focus light through the pupil. Photoreceptors in the retina convert that light into neural signals, which leave the eye in the optic nerve.

02

The visual pathway

Nasal retinal fibres cross at the optic chiasm. Information from each visual field is combined and carried through the optic tracts, lateral geniculate nucleus and optic radiations to the primary visual cortex.

VISUAL DEVELOPMENT

Visual Development

Normal milestones help clinicians recognise when a child needs further assessment or referral.

01

How the world becomes clearer

Acuity, contrast, colour and depth perception improve rapidly from birth, then continue to mature throughout childhood.

02

Development in the Womb: Pregnancy

Inside the womb, the unborn baby is surrounded by gentle and calming sound, including their mother's heartbeat and voices and sounds from the world outside.

As pregnancy reaches its final stages, the baby's eyes can open inside the womb.

While they can't yet see images or details, they can sense changes in light.

03

Vision at Birth

At birth vision is very unlike that of an adult. A newborn is 'blind' with poor visual acuity of around 6/600. They do not perceive much colour and have low contrast sensitivity. They have no stereovision and their eyes may squint now and again.

They will however look directly at faces at very close range, and squeeze their eyes shut to bright lights. Their vision from now is going to develop very rapidly and become like an adult by 8 years of age.

04

2 Weeks

Within two weeks vision has improved hugely. They can now see some detail, especially if things are up close. There is no colour and what they do see is flat, because their eyes are not yet always straight and working together for depth perception.

Sometimes their eyes will drift in or out for a short while. This is called squinting. This should stop happening by three months.

05

2 Weeks - 1 Month

By one month colour vision is starting to develop. They will be much more visually interested especially to faces. They will also start to try to follow things that are moving.

They will become more and more aware of familiar sounds including well known voices like their family. And start to join up what they hear with what they see.

06

1 - 3 Months

By three months their eyes should always be straight and working together to create 3D vision. This new depth perception helps them reach for things more accurately. They will be able to follow things that are moving faster but it is hard to switch fixation from one face or object to another quickly.

They like to look at only one thing at a time. They may also start to make their first meaningful sounds.

07

3 - 6 Months

An infant with normally developing vision will become excited at seeing a drinking cup or spoon and can routinely fix and follow a face at a distance of 1-2 metres as well as be able to differentiate between familiar and unfamiliar faces by both their faces and voices.

They can also start to see things that are further away. Driven by curiosity, they will begin to reach for things.

08

6 - 12 Months

At 6 to 12 months of age the visual acuity is improving and is around 6/36. The distance at which faces are looked at and recognised has extended to 2 metres. All elements of vision are improving with more accurate reach and grasp.

At this age they are becoming more interested in images in books and on television, although real objects are more engaging encouraging them to crawl and around their first birthday, walk towards objects of interest. Their babblings are developing, and sounds will become first words at this age as well.

09

12 - 18 Months

By 18 months, vision is quite adult like although still a bit blurry at visual acuity of around 6/18. They will now be able to recognise their name and be using more and more words.

10

18 Months - 8 Years

After 18 months of age vision still needs several years to mature. Normal adult visual acuity will be formed by the age of 8.

11

After 8 Years

After the age of 8 the visual system may not improve well to treatment such as cataract surgery. Identification of disease and referral for treatment is therefore best done as early in life as possible.

It can be seen that a huge amount of development occurs between birth and 18 months of age and continues more slowly throughout the rest of childhood. During this period it is essential that the child is healthy with a disease free visual system and provided with a stimulating and educationally nourishing environment.

Early identification of disease with referral for treatment is important to help maximise both visual and general development.

HISTORY TAKING

Vision Loss and Red Eye

Use a calm, structured consultation to define the symptom, identify red flags and agree a safe plan.

01

Open the consultation

  • Wash your hands and wear appropriate PPE
  • Introduce yourself: name and role
  • Confirm the patient's name and date of birth
  • Explain that you would like to take a history
  • Gain consent to proceed
02

Explore the presenting complaint

Start with open questions: “What has brought you in today?” and “Tell me about the issues you have been experiencing.” Allow time for the patient to answer without interruption.

Explore further with focused closed questions. When there are several complaints, agree priorities and set a shared agenda.

Start open, then use closed questions for detail.
03

Key ophthalmic symptoms

Visual disturbance
Change in vision, blurring or loss of vision
Red eye
Redness of the white or part of the eye
Discharge or watering
Type, colour and amount
Grittiness or dryness
Foreign-body sensation or dry eyes
Itching
Persistent eye rubbing
Photophobia
Discomfort in bright light
Swelling or tenderness
Around or on the eye
04

Red eye and trauma

Ask about:

  • Onset and duration
  • One eye or both
  • Pain or discomfort
  • Discharge: watery, sticky or yellow
  • Itching, grittiness or foreign-body sensation
  • Photophobia
  • Vision change
  • Contact lens use
  • Mechanism and time of injury
  • Chemical or high-velocity injury
  • Systemic symptoms: fever, cough or rash
Red flags

Severe pain, reduced vision, chemical or sharp-object trauma, periorbital or globe injury, or contact-lens use with pain or reduced vision require urgent assessment.

05

Vision loss

Ask about onset, duration, one or both eyes, partial or total loss, pain, associated headache, diplopia, flashes or floaters, and triggers or relieving factors.

Sudden, painless

Retinal artery or vein occlusion, stroke, AION, vitreous haemorrhage, retinal detachment

Gradual, painless

Cataract, refractive error, glaucoma, AMD, tumour, nutritional optic neuropathy

Painful

Acute glaucoma, optic neuritis, uveitis, keratitis, endophthalmitis, AION

Red flags

Sudden loss, pain, headache, flashes or floaters, temporal headache or jaw claudication require urgent assessment.

Consider corneal abrasion, uveitis, acute glaucoma, optic neuritis, orbital cellulitis and migraine.

07

Systemic and ophthalmic history

Systemic history
  • Medical conditions: diabetes, hypertension, autoimmune disease
  • Medications, including eye drops
  • Allergies
  • Smoking and alcohol
  • Relevant family history
Ophthalmic history
  • Previous eye conditions or surgery
  • Glasses or contact-lens use
  • Last eye test
  • Similar episodes in the past
These factors may affect risk, diagnosis and management.
08

Close the consultation safely

  • Summarise the key points discussed
  • Explain the plan and what happens next
  • Check whether the patient has questions
  • Thank the patient and close professionally
  • Safety-net: advise when and how to seek urgent help if symptoms worsen
  • Document the history accurately
  • Show empathy
  • Listen actively
  • Maintain eye contact
  • Use open and relaxed body language
  • Do not interrupt
  • Build rapport
  • Signpost
  • Summarise regularly

EXAMINATION TOOLS

Arclight

A compact, solar-powered tool for examining the front and back of the eye in almost any setting.

01

Traditional examination tools

Eye examination is often taught with multiple large, expensive or power-dependent instruments. Access to this equipment varies greatly between settings.

02

A pocket examination device

The Arclight combines an ophthalmoscope, loupe, light source and useful near-vision and colour targets in a robust handheld form.

03

Designed through iteration

Successive prototypes refined the size, controls, optics and charging system into a practical clinical device.

04

The teaching kit

The kit can include a case, visual acuity and reading charts, examination accessories and charging equipment for training and field use.

05

Know the controls

Identify the loupe and otoscope end, ophthalmoscope aperture, light switch, colour strip, lenses, pupil gauge, ruler, fixation targets, solar panel and USB-C charging point before examining a patient.

EYE DISEASE & BLINDNESS · HIGH V LOW RESOURCE SETTINGS

Barriers

What can stop a patient taking the first step—or completing the journey to treatment?

01

Cost and competing needs

Money and food come before travel or treatment for many families. Direct costs, lost work and repeated visits can all interrupt the patient journey.

02

Distance and transport

Poor roads, lack of a vehicle and distance from specialist care can turn a simple appointment into a major journey.

03

Home, language and culture

Responsibilities at home, communication barriers and cultural expectations influence whether, when and where a patient seeks care.

04

Barriers exist everywhere

These barriers are not limited to low-resource countries. Within high-resource settings, people in lower socioeconomic groups are also less likely or less able to access eye care.

EYE DISEASE & BLINDNESS · HIGH V LOW RESOURCE SETTINGS

Diagnosis of Eye Disease

Use a consistent sequence, then connect findings to the major causes of visual impairment.

01

A structured route to diagnosis

Start broadly, then move from visual function to the front and back of the eye.

  1. History
  2. Visual acuity
  3. Pupils
  4. Pressure
  5. Anterior segment
  6. Fundal reflex
  7. Fundoscopy
02

Refractive error

History, distance and near visual acuity, and improvement with a pinhole can identify refractive error as a likely cause of visual impairment.

03

Cataract

Cataract can affect adults and babies. Anterior segment and fundal reflex examination help identify an opaque lens or an abnormal red reflex.

04

Glaucoma

A cupped optic disc can be seen on fundoscopy. Measuring eye pressure can provide additional support for the diagnosis.

05

Retinal disease

Age-related macular degeneration and diabetic retinopathy are assessed with visual acuity and direct ophthalmoscopy. AMD is a leading cause of blindness in high-income countries; diabetic retinopathy is a rapidly rising cause in working-age populations in emerging economies.

06

Trachoma

Trachoma is the leading infectious cause of blindness globally. Anterior segment examination reveals the characteristic lid and corneal changes.

07

Trauma and infection

Late presentation, a longer patient journey and poor access to treatment can turn anterior segment trauma or infection into permanent visual loss.

08

Retinoblastoma

Retinoblastoma is rare but life-threatening. Outcomes are much better with early diagnosis and access to treatment. Fundal red reflex examination at birth and during immunisation can reveal a white reflex that needs urgent referral.

MEDICAL STUDENTS WORKSHOP · INTRODUCTION

Objectives

GMC-driven outcomes for focused history, examination, diagnosis and safe first-line management.

01

History and examination

Take a focused ophthalmic history for red eye, trauma, diplopia, transient symptoms and acute, subacute or gradual loss of vision; use the findings to form an appropriate differential diagnosis and management plan.

Perform a structured eye examination: visual assessment, anterior segment examination with a slit lamp or simple magnification, fundal reflex and direct ophthalmoscopy—including optic disc colour, contour and cupping—visual fields, squint assessment, and recognition of papilloedema or an abnormal fundal reflex in a child.

02

Vision, development and screening

Understand normal visual development during early childhood and its relationship to motor and language milestones; define amblyopia and describe the principles of testing visual acuity to recognise myopia, hypermetropia and presbyopia.

Recall diabetic eye screening and pre-school vision screening; recognise common causes of low vision, understand visual impairment registration and the role of the Eye Clinic Liaison Officer, and summarise UK visual standards for driving.

03

Red eye, anterior segment disease and trauma

Differentiate infective, inflammatory, allergic and pressure-related causes of a red eye, including conjunctivitis, keratitis, endophthalmitis, episcleritis, scleritis, uveitis, allergic conjunctivitis, acute angle-closure glaucoma and hyphaema.

Recognise ocular trauma and initiate supervised basic management, including corneal abrasion or ulcer, subtarsal or corneal foreign body, chemical injury, penetrating injury and blow-out fracture; use fluorescein appropriately and identify features requiring urgent referral.

04

Change or loss of vision

Recognise gradual visual loss from cataract, age-related macular degeneration, chronic glaucoma, diabetic or hypertensive eye disease and refractive causes; provide advice and explain cataract surgery, including indications, expected benefits and important risks.

Recognise urgent acute or subacute visual loss and transient symptoms, including acute glaucoma, retinal artery or vein occlusion, ischaemic optic neuropathy, vitreous haemorrhage, retinal detachment, wet AMD, optic neuritis, amaurosis fugax, migraine, papilloedema and functional visual loss; interpret the significance of flashes and floaters.

05

Ocular motility and other common eye conditions

Assess diplopia clinically and formulate a differential and management plan for third, fourth and sixth cranial nerve palsies, thyroid eye disease, myasthenia gravis and orbital trauma.

Describe the presentation, causes, risk factors and basic management of common eyelid, ocular surface and intraocular conditions, including blepharitis, dry eye, meibomian cyst, basal cell carcinoma, herpes zoster ophthalmicus, subconjunctival haemorrhage, choroidal naevus and melanoma.
