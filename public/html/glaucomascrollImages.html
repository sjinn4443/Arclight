What is glaucoma

Glaucoma damages the optic nerve and causes progressive loss of vision.

01

The optic nerve carries vision to the brain

Glaucoma is a disease of the optic nerve.

The optic nerve is a bundle of more than one million tiny nerve fibres.

It carries visual signals from the eye to the brain.

EyeOptic nerveBrain
02

Nerve fibres die faster than normal

  • As we get older, some of these nerve fibres slowly die.
  • In glaucoma, they die faster than normal.
  • This causes loss of vision.
Normal ageing
Glaucoma

nerve fibre loss

03

Vision loss starts at the edges

  • At first, vision is lost at the edges (side vision).
  • Later, the loss moves towards the centre.
  • If not treated, this can lead to blindness.

Types of glaucoma

The workshop focuses mainly on POAG and compares it with ACAG where useful.

01

There are several different types

There are several different types of glaucoma.

  1. Primary Open Angle Glaucoma (POAG)
  2. Acute closed angle glaucoma (ACAG)
  3. Secondary Glaucoma
  4. Congenital Glaucoma

Primary open angle glaucoma (POAG) is the most common form and is the main cause of untreatable blindness globally.

POAGACAGSecondaryCongenital
02

What causes POAG?

We do not know the exact cause.

But we know who is at higher risk:

  • Age over 40 years
  • Black African ancestry
  • Family history (parent or sibling with glaucoma)
  • High eye pressure (intra-ocular pressure, IOP)
  • Short sight (myopia)

POAG and ACAG

Typical history findings separate slow POAG from acute angle closure.

01

History: Typical Findings in POAG

  • Vision loss is slow (months to years)
  • Usually no pain
  • Eye looks white (not red)
  • Higher risk in Black African patients
  • Higher risk if a parent or sibling has glaucoma
  • More common over age 40
  • More common in people who are short sighted

ACAGfastred painful eyehalos
02

History: Typical Findings in ACAG

  • Vision loss is fast (hours to days)
  • Eye is painful and red
  • Patient may see halos around lights
  • May have had similar attacks before
  • Often long sighted (needs glasses for far and near)
  • Higher risk in some Asian populations
  • More common in older female people
  • Especially with cataract
  • Fast change
  • Red painful eye
  • Halos or previous attacks
  • Refer urgently

Intro & Vision loss

Central acuity may look normal early, while side vision is already being lost.

01

2. Visual Acuity

  • In early glaucoma, central vision is usually normal.
  • Side vision is lost first.
  • Later, central vision is affected.
  • This is often when patients present late.
  • By then, damage is severe.
  • This is why active screening is essential.
02

Visual Acuity / Visual Field Loss

Peripheral visual world becomes blurry first. Central vision may remain clear until late, then it can also be lost.

The pattern can progress from side vision loss to tunnel vision and then severe overall loss.

Pupil reactions

A reduced direct response can suggest retinal or optic nerve damage.

01

Pupils should constrict to bright light

Pupils constrict fast and fully to bright light if the retina and optic nerve are both healthy.

If the retina or optic nerve are damaged the pupil will not constrict so well.

The optic nerve wears out in glaucoma and so in those affected the pupil may not constrict so well.

normalreducedabsent
02

Classify the response

In screening for and diagnosing glaucoma it is important to be able to test the pupil response to bright light correctly and interpret the response. This can be broadly classified as normal, reduced or absent.

If the response is reduced or absent then optic nerve damage from glaucoma maybe present.

Front of eye findings associated with raised intra-ocular pressure which can lead to 'secondary' glaucoma:

Feeling for high IOP in ACAG

Finger palpation can help detect very high IOP in suspected ACAG.

01

Feeling for high IOP in ACAG

It is possible to detect high IOP using your fingertips.

The accuracy is better if the examiner is familiar with this examination method.

Take time to practice it first on yourself and then on your colleagues.

If you do not detect anything abnormal, the eye pressure may still be dangerously high.

If the history or symptoms suggest glaucoma you must refer them to a centre where their IOP can be accurately assessed.

Place both index fingers on the upper eyelid

Ask the patient to close her or his eyes and look down.

Place the tips of both index fingers on the closed upper eyelid.

03

Palpate through the closed eyelid

Use the tips of both index fingers to gently feel the eye through the closed upper lid.

The test is only a rough screen for very high pressure and does not replace accurate IOP measurement.

  • Closed upper lid only
  • Rough screen, not measurement
04

Compare firmness between the two eyes

First gently pressing on the eye with one finger then with the other and repeat.

A normal eye should feel a bit like a tomato that is just ripe: not solid, nor very soft.

An eye with high IOP will feel firmer and hard.

It is important to compare the two eyes with one other.

An eye with very high IOP will feel firmer and more solid.

Introduction

The red reflex rules out media opacity; fundoscopy examines the optic nerve.

01

Fundal Reflex and Fundoscopy

The fundal reflex allows quick assessment of the potential other causes of loss of vision.

If visual acuity is reduced then excluding other causes such as corneal scar, cataract or bleeding into the vitreous are important.

If the fundal reflex looks fine with clear media yet the vision is reduced then glaucoma is possible.

Refractive error should be excluded by testing near and distance visual acuity using a pin hole.

The neuro-retinal rim and central cup are the key optic nerve structures.

01

The healthy optic nerve

A healthy optic nerve on fundoscopy typically looks like this:

The disc has a pink neuro-retinal rim and a smaller central pale cup.

neuro-retinal rimcentral pale cup
02

Rim and cup

  • The neuro-retinal rim is made up of 1 to 1.75 million fine nerves that run from the retina to the brain.
  • The central pale area is called the 'cup' because from side on it looks like a cup.
  • Rim: nerve fibres to the brain
  • Cup: central pale area
  • Screening looks for rim loss
Cupping

GLAUCOMA WORKSHOP

Cupping

As glaucoma progresses, the neuro-retinal rim becomes thinner and the cup appears larger.

01

The optic nerve and cupping

  • In glaucoma the large collection of fine nerves that make up the optic nerve wear out and die early.
  • This neuro-retinal rim is made up of these nerves.
  • As glaucoma progresses the neuro-retinal rim becomes thinner.
  • This makes the central pale cup look bigger.
  • This is why when screening for glaucoma we look for 'cupping' or a 'cupped' disc.

03

History

  • Slow, painless loss of side vision over months or years
  • Bumping into objects or people, especially in low light
  • Difficulty walking outdoors or at night
  • In very late stages: only central vision left ("tunnel vision"), or severe overall blur
  • May have family history of blindness or glaucoma
  • Older age (often >40), but can be younger
  • Sometimes already functionally blind in one eye when first seen
04

Visual Acuity

  • Reduced distance vision, especially if disease is very advanced or combined with cataract
  • Near vision may still be reasonable early on, then worsens
  • Marked asymmetry between eyes is common (one eye much worse)
  • Visual acuity can be surprisingly good despite severe field loss so normal acuity does NOT exclude advanced glaucoma.

Visual Fields

  • Large superior and/or inferior field defects
  • Loss in both upper and lower fields
  • Progression to tunnel vision
  • With confrontation test:
  • Fingers or red target not seen in upper and/or lower quadrants
  • Peripheral targets missed first
  • Central target may still be seen until very late
