ABCDE-SU

Uneven shape or colour. If you draw an imaginary line through the lesion, the two halves do not broadly match.

Edge not smooth or even: uneven, blurred, notched or ragged.

Mixed shades in the same lesion: light brown, dark brown, black, pink, red, white or blue-grey.

Use as a guide rather than a rule. Around 7 mm or larger, or getting bigger, is more concerning.

New or changing lesion. Size, shape, colour, surface, bleeding, itch or pain can all count as change.

New itch, bleeding, oozing or crusting not explained by scratching, trauma or another clear cause.

Compare with the patient's other moles or skin lesions. This one stands out as clearly different.

Chaos + Clues

Compare the two halves of the dermoscopy image. Chaos means uneven colour or internal pattern from side to side.

An irregular outline alone is not chaos if the inside looks organised.

If chaos: check clues

Grey, blue or white areas/lines, or black dots/clods at the lesion edge.

Off-centre blank-looking area, thick dark network or thick branched lines.

One-sided streaks or pseudopods at the edge. Think active growth at part of the border, not an even rim.

Mixed vessel patterns or chaotic nail pigment.

Exception

These escalate even without clear chaos: changing pigmented lesion in an adult, pigmented nodule, grey on the head/neck or palm/sole ridge pigment.

Rash triage

Darker skin: check darkening, purple/grey change, warmth or swelling.

New or clearly worsening rashes are more concerning than stable long-standing rashes. Check when it started and whether it is spreading.

Main visible surface pattern. Blisters/pustules contain fluid or pus; raised bumps are palpable; flat patches are colour change without a raised surface.

Eczema/dermatitis is often itchy, dry, patchy or flexural. Psoriasis-type plaques are usually well-defined with thicker scale.

Severe itch can suggest active inflammation, infestation or urticaria. It is a context clue rather than a diagnosis on its own.

Obvious inflammation or change. On darker skin this may look purple-grey, darker than nearby skin or swollen rather than bright red.

Same day: widespread or rapidly spreading rash, painful rash, blistering rash, fever or patient unwell.

Emergency: airway or facial swelling, breathing symptoms, mucosal involvement, skin peeling, non-blanching rash with illness or sepsis concern.

Wood's lamp guide

Technique: use a blacked out room. Creams, deodorant, lint and surface products can fluoresce.

Blue-green - M. tinea capitis

Supports Microsporum tinea capitis. A negative Wood's lamp does not exclude fungal scalp infection.

Yellow-orange - Pityriasis versicolor

Supports pityriasis versicolor when there is a scaly rash that fits the clinical picture.

Coral-red - Erythrasma

Supports erythrasma, often in body folds. The fluorescence comes from porphyrins made by Corynebacterium.

Bright blue-white - Vitiligo

Vitiligo often looks sharply brighter under Wood's lamp. This can help show the edge and extent of depigmented patches.

Orange-red - Acne porphyrins

May be seen with acne porphyrins. Use as a supportive sign, not a stand-alone diagnosis.

White - head lice nits

Nits may show white fluorescence. Confirm by looking for attached eggs on hair shafts close to the scalp.
